New AI Venture Studio Arsenal Sets Up Shop in Lawrenceville

Two Carnegie Mellon grads launched Arsenal AI to build startups in exchange for equity, with a goal of keeping Pittsburgh's next wave of AI companies rooted in the city.

By Rachel Osei September 3, 2026 3 min read

Arsenal AI held its official launch celebration on Aug. 6 inside a Downtown venue with red curtains and a room full of the people it hopes to keep in Pittsburgh: engineers, founders, and the kind of AI talent that too often lands job offers in San Francisco or Boston instead. The new venture studio, cofounded by Carnegie Mellon graduates Jacob Rayburg and Chris Quimby, is betting that the fastest way to grow the region's tech economy is to stop asking entrepreneurs to build alone.

Arsenal is operating out of Field Day, the coworking space in Lawrenceville, while its founders scout a larger home base for later this year. The studio's model is straightforward on paper and unusual in practice: instead of writing a check and stepping back, Arsenal trades its own engineering team's time for equity in the companies it works with, layering in cash investment through SAFE notes when it makes sense. Unlike many venture studios, the founders say Arsenal does not take ownership of the intellectual property its portfolio companies build.

"We're looking for leaders of industries that have great ideas that are primed to innovate, and we want to apply emerging, cutting-edge technology to make that innovation possible," Rayburg said. "Though it's deeply related, we do think of ourselves as a venture platform and AI consultancy."

Four Startups, One Bet on Staying Local

Rayburg and Quimby met through CMU's Swartz Center for Entrepreneurship, and both bring technical backgrounds in deploying software at large, data-heavy organizations. That experience shapes who they choose to back. Four startups are already working inside Arsenal: SymphonyMR, a medtech company building virtual reality goggles that let surgeons view and manipulate imaging like CT scans mid-procedure; Assembuild, an AI platform that helps housing developers navigate zoning and permitting; Fathom Shield Solutions, an IT and cybersecurity modernization firm serving military and federal clients; and Heirwise, a legal-tech startup that helps people execute wills.

The pitch behind Arsenal goes beyond any single portfolio company. Rayburg and Quimby argue that AI tools now let a small team start a company in half the time and for half the money it used to take, which they see as an opening to keep more of that early-stage activity anchored in Pittsburgh rather than losing it to coastal hubs once a startup needs to scale.

"We want to keep the most innovative, exciting companies in Pittsburgh, because they're already being created here."

Jacob Rayburg, cofounder, Arsenal AI

The timing lines up with a broader run of momentum for Pittsburgh's AI and robotics economy. The region's startups pulled in a record $1.7 billion in the first quarter of the year, AI hardware maker Hellbender is doubling its footprint at Hazelwood Green with plans to add 500 local jobs over five years, and the inaugural StartUP PGH week arrives Sept. 14 to 18 with programming from Carnegie Mellon, Innovation Works, and more than a dozen partner organizations across the region.

What It Means for Pittsburgh

Arsenal AI is a small operation for now, four portfolio companies working out of a Lawrenceville coworking space. But it is also a bet on a specific theory of how Pittsburgh keeps its edge in artificial intelligence: not just by producing talent at CMU and Pitt, but by giving that talent a reason to build here instead of somewhere else. If Rayburg and Quimby are right, the studio's next move to a larger space later this year will be the first of many signs that the model is working.
