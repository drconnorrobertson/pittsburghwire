Restaurant Week Draws 77 Kitchens Across Pittsburgh

The summer edition of Pittsburgh Restaurant Week runs through Aug. 16 with 77 participating eateries and 10 first-time newcomers, turning the slowest month on the calendar into an argument for the city's dining rooms.

By The Pittsburgh Wire Editorial Staff August 13, 2026 4 min read

August has never been kind to restaurants. Vacations empty the neighborhoods, the heat keeps people out of dining rooms, and the checks that carried a kitchen through spring simply stop arriving. Which is precisely why Pittsburgh Restaurant Week lands where it does. The summer edition opened Monday and runs through Aug. 16, with a preview weekend that gave early diners a head start, and this year the lineup has swelled to 77 participating eateries stretching from Downtown to Mt. Lebanon to Finleyville. Ten of them are participating for the first time.

The premise has not changed in more than a decade: multi-course, fixed-price menus, most of them $60 and under, built to lower the barrier between a curious diner and a restaurant they have driven past a hundred times without stopping. Brian McCollum, who directs the event, frames it as a matchmaking exercise rather than a discount.

"The mission has always been that you find a restaurant name that you're unfamiliar with, or you rediscover your favorite restaurant."
Brian McCollum, Director, Pittsburgh Restaurant Week

Among the newcomers, Harry & Fritz Downtown may be the clearest illustration of what the week is for. Owner Joe Beckham opened the restaurant in June and named it after his grandfather and great-uncle. Its three-course dinner runs $39.26 and leans unapologetically retro: a petit filet mignon with béarnaise, Faroe Islands salmon, and a "chicken two ways" plate of pan-seared breast and strudel drawn from the old supper-club playbook. Beckham's stated goal is simply introduction. Sushi Kim 2 Downtown joins with a $20.26 roll-and-dish combo, alongside South Side Mexican restaurant Teocalli and Finleyville's Latitude 48, a project created by a group of pilots.

Old Rooms, New Arguments

The returning restaurants are making a different case. At fl.2 inside the Fairmont Pittsburgh, steps from Market Square, a $45.26 prix fixe built on local summer produce runs from chicken paillard with squash ratatouille through corn agnolotti to a peach galette with basil ice cream. Public relations manager Christopher Ramirez says the week exists partly to correct a misconception. "A lot of people have the perception fl.2 is only for guests," he said. "But the restaurant is open to everybody and right in the middle of everything in the Cultural District."

In North Braddock, Asti's Italian Steakhouse is using its $45.26 menu to show its hand: chicken saltimbocca and house-made pappardelle with braised cinghiale, the wild boar soaked 36 hours in Chianti and aromatics, a dish traced to the Torsana village his family left generations ago. Chef and owner Francis Asti opened inside the Grand View Golf Club six and a half years ago and is still working to convince the neighborhood the door is open to the public. "I know everybody says this: live-fired, from scratch," he said. "But in our case, that's true."

At Luma in Mt. Lebanon, Restaurant Week's longest-running participant, owner Greg Ackerman is candid about the math. August is slow, and the week helps. His $45.26 menu pairs the signature short rib and stuffed crab with new desserts, including a tropical acai sorbet, but his marketing plan remains the oldest one there is. "We're a little neighborhoody place," he said. "Marketing is when somebody comes in, they have a good meal, and they go tell 10 people." Elsewhere on the returning roster: Paris 66 in East Liberty, Mitchell's Fish Market in Mt. Lebanon at $39.26, and LeMont on Mount Washington, whose $55.26 menu closes with a burnt almond panna cotta built as a nod to the Prantl's cake.

What makes this year's edition worth noticing is not the discount but the count. Seventy-seven kitchens choosing to open their books during the leanest stretch of the year, ten of them brand new to the exercise, is a measure of a dining economy that still believes a full room in August is worth chasing. Pittsburgh has spent a decade earning a reputation as a city where a serious restaurant can survive outside the marquee neighborhoods. A week in which North Braddock, Finleyville, Mt. Lebanon, and Downtown all sit on the same list is the clearest proof of that claim anyone is likely to publish this summer.
