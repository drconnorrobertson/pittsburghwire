Allegheny County Invests $11.5 Million Across 94 Community Projects

County Executive Sara Innamorato announced a sweeping round of neighborhood improvement grants this week, directing federal and county dollars toward sidewalks, small business training, housing preservation, and long-overdue blight demolition across the Pittsburgh region.

By The Pittsburgh Wire Staff July 29, 2026 4 min read
Pittsburgh

Allegheny County's latest round of community development grants will fund infrastructure repairs and blight removal projects across dozens of municipalities.

Allegheny County announced Monday that it will distribute $11.5 million across 94 projects to improve neighborhoods, repair aging infrastructure, and clear blighted properties that have sat vacant for years across the Pittsburgh region. County Executive Sara Innamorato said the investment reflects a belief that thriving neighborhoods are not a luxury — they are the precondition for everything else the county wants to accomplish.

The bulk of the money, nearly $10 million, flows through the federally funded Community Development Block Grant program and will support 72 distinct community initiatives. Projects range from street reconstruction and water line replacements to accessibility improvements like curb ramps and a professional development series for residents looking to open home-based child care businesses. Together, they represent one of the most diverse single-round uses of CDBG funding the county has deployed in recent memory.

By the Numbers
$11.5M Total investment announced by Allegheny County Executive Sara Innamorato on July 27, 2026
94 Total community improvement projects funded across the Pittsburgh region
72 Community development initiatives funded through the federal CDBG program
22 Demolition and blight remediation projects in residential and commercial corridors

The remaining $2 million is set aside for the county's blight removal program, which will fund 22 demolition and remediation projects targeting long-vacant structures in both residential blocks and commercial corridors. Blight removal has been a persistent priority for municipalities across Allegheny County, where abandoned properties can suppress home values, discourage new investment, and create safety hazards for neighboring residents. This round clears a meaningful backlog for communities that have waited years for action.

"Behind every one of these 96 projects are real people, families looking for a safe and affordable place to live, neighbors who deserve streets and sidewalks they can count on."

Sara Innamorato, Allegheny County Executive

Innamorato underscored the human dimension of what can sometimes read as a technical funding announcement. "Residents who have watched blighted properties sit next door for far too long," she said in a statement, calling these investments the groundwork for "opportunity and growth well into the future." The framing matters: community development spending often gets reduced to line items, but each project on this list represents a specific street, a specific block, a specific set of neighbors who have been waiting.

A Wide Reach Across the Region

The diversity of the funded projects speaks to the breadth of need across Allegheny County's 130-plus municipalities, which range from dense urban neighborhoods to small boroughs where a single water main replacement can reshape a street's viability for decades. Among the funded categories are sidewalk repairs designed to connect residents to transit and commercial areas, housing preservation work for low-income homeowners, and the home-based child care professional development series, which addresses both workforce access and the county's ongoing shortage of licensed child care providers.

Lauren Connelly, Allegheny County's director of economic development, noted that the investments are calibrated to meet communities where they are. "Whether we're helping a municipality repair critical infrastructure, preserve affordable housing, or remove long-standing blight, these investments are about giving communities the resources they need to move their priorities forward," Connelly said. The CDBG program, which has funded local improvements nationwide since 1974, gives counties like Allegheny significant latitude in directing dollars toward the highest-need projects within federal guidelines.

How to Apply

The county will open applications for both the community development and blight removal grant programs soon. Organizations, municipalities, and government groups interested in applying must attend a mandatory training session on August 12 before submitting. The session is designed to walk applicants through eligibility requirements, documentation standards, and the competitive scoring criteria that determine which projects are funded each cycle.

For Pittsburgh and its surrounding communities, this round of investment arrives at a moment when neighborhood-level revitalization is increasingly seen not as a standalone government function but as infrastructure for the region's broader economic growth. New businesses need accessible streetscapes. New residents need housing stock that hasn't been undermined by adjacent vacancy. New workers need child care. Monday's announcement advances all of it at once, in nearly a hundred communities that are ready to get to work.
