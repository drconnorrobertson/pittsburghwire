Read Pittsburgh Air-Quality Monitor Reports in Context

A Pittsburgh air-quality report is easier to understand when you identify what each number describes before comparing it with another number. A pollutant concentration, an Air Quality Index value, a forecast and an annual summary can all appear in the same research session. Their labels, dates, geographic scope and verification status matter. A familiar station name or a recently updated webpage does not make every displayed value a current reading of the air at your own location.

This guide explains how to read those distinctions using official discovery and methodology pages plus six original fictional written cases. It does not report current Pittsburgh conditions, inspect individual monitoring records, calculate an official AQI or assess anyone's exposure or health. All example readings, station identifiers, flags and dates are invented. For current conditions and health-related activity guidance, use the relevant official reports and AirNow's published guidance. The examples here are data-reading exercises, not instructions that an outing or location is safe.

Choose the report that matches the question

Start by writing the question in plain language. You might want the current official public communication for an area, a particular monitor's historical measurements or a longer-term summary. These are different requests. A report designed for one purpose may provide context for another without answering it. If your question is about what is being reported now, preserve the report's time and area. If it concerns historical measurements, identify the series, monitor, pollutant and period before drawing a conclusion about change.

Allegheny County's Monitored Data page links separate hourly data, current AQI, forecast and dispersion information, and annual reports. It explicitly describes the hourly data as unverified until reviewed by qualified Air Quality staff and notes that disruptions or equipment malfunction can produce erroneous data. It separately describes annual reports as analyses of verified data. Those statements help distinguish products and status; they do not establish that a particular reading is correct, incorrect, current or personally representative.

The EPA's Download Daily Data page offers daily summary statistics by monitor and filters such as pollutant, year and geographic area. That is another useful starting point for a historical question. Reading its interface description does not mean a particular query has been run or that an individual row has been inspected. Keep a discovery source, a selected dataset and an examined record separate in your notes. This guide reviewed discovery descriptions, not a live result set or a complete inventory of local monitoring observations.

Keep concentration and index values in different fields

A concentration describes an amount using a stated unit and measurement definition. An index is a different reported quantity. Do not compare them merely because both are numbers on an air-quality page. Preserve the full column heading, pollutant label and unit with an extracted value. If a screenshot removes the heading, the number alone may not support the interpretation you want. Return to the source definition before assigning it a category or comparing it with another display.

Fictional case A supplies two separate entries: a PM2.5 concentration labeled twelve micrograms per cubic meter and a different field labeled AQI twelve. The matching numeral does not make the entries interchangeable. This case supplies no conversion between them, no official breakpoint table and no actual environmental observation. Its supported conclusion is simply that the quantity labels differ. Neither invented entry should be used to characterize today's Pittsburgh air, decide a health category or determine whether a specific person should change an activity.

AirNow describes the U.S. AQI as a public communication tool and distinguishes its daily index, forecasts and NowCast reporting. Its NowCast explanation describes separate algorithms relating hourly monitor readings to current AQI for ozone and particle pollution. This guide does not implement those algorithms. In particular, the simple arithmetic examples later on are not shortcuts to an official AQI. Keep the official product definition attached to an official value rather than replacing it with an improvised calculation based on a convenient subset of numbers.

Separate the observation period from the webpage update

A report can display several relevant times. The observation period describes when a value applies. Publication or processing time describes a later reporting step. Your access time records when you viewed the source. A page's general update label may describe the page or resource, rather than the newest valid observation. Copy the role attached to each time and retain any stated time basis. If the source does not specify that basis, keep it unresolved instead of silently assigning your computer's timezone.

Fictional case B supplies an observation label of 08:00 and a report label of 09:15 on the same invented date, June 12, 2030. The example explicitly declares both as fixed UTC clock labels. The difference is seventy-five minutes. That arithmetic measures the separation of the supplied labels only. It does not establish a typical reporting delay, a current local observation or a prediction for conditions after the observation. The date is fictional and the fixed time basis is not a claim about the format used by any actual county source.

When comparing two real reports, check whether their time labels refer to the same kind of period. A single observation, an hourly summary, a daily summary and a prediction can carry different temporal meanings. Matching the calendar date is not enough to establish comparability. Preserve any averaging interval and end-of-period convention the actual documentation provides. If the convention remains unclear, describe the comparison as unresolved rather than inventing an exact temporal match from the displayed clock alone.

Read the station identifier as part of the observation

Keep the monitor or station identifier with every value that depends on it. A geographic label in a report can identify a site, an administrative area or a public reporting area. Those are different scopes. Do not turn a monitor's label into a finding about every address with a similar place name. A map can help locate a reported site, but it does not establish the conditions inside a particular building or the exposure experienced by a person moving through several places.

Fictional case E describes two entries at Station EX-AIR-01 and one at Station EX-AIR-02. The first station has entries for PM2.5 and ozone; the second has a PM2.5 entry. There are three supplied rows, two station identifiers and two pollutant labels. Those counts describe the invented table only. The two entries from EX-AIR-01 are not duplicate PM2.5 observations merely because the station identifier matches. Conversely, sharing a pollutant label does not make the entries from different stations the same observation.

Define the identity of the record before trying to deduplicate or join tables. Depending on the actual source contract, fields such as station, pollutant, time, method or summary period may matter. This guide does not supply a universal county record key. It illustrates why a proposed key must be checked against the source's definitions and the intended operation. Removing a row because one label repeats can discard a different measurement. Retaining every repeated-looking row without review can also obscure what the table represents.

Check units before comparing values

A unit is part of the value's meaning. Keep its spelling and scale with the number during a handoff to a spreadsheet, an AI assistant or a written summary. A column named concentration without a unit can conceal a mismatch. A header carried by the original file can be lost when someone copies only selected rows. Before comparing values, establish that they concern compatible quantities and a common unit basis. Even after that check, the observation period and pollutant must still match the comparison you intend.

Fictional case D supplies a ratio labeled 0.008 parts per million and another label expressed in parts per billion. Under the explicitly declared ratio-unit conversion, multiplying 0.008 by one thousand gives eight parts per billion. That is a change of numerical representation for the authored ratio. It is not an actual pollutant reading, a conversion from a gas ratio to mass concentration, an AQI calculation or a determination about regulatory compliance. No environmental sample, temperature or pressure measurement is supplied.

The WPRDC Allegheny County Air Quality catalog includes resource-specific notes about unit differences among stations and changes over time, as well as standardization in particular resources. Read those notes in relation to the actual resource you select. Do not generalize one note into a claim that every historical file, PDF, dashboard and export uses the same unit. This guide did not audit those resources or reproduce a historical conversion. The catalog's descriptions are a reason to inspect the relevant fields before making a comparison.

Preserve missing observations instead of inventing zeros

A missing value needs its own state. It can mean that information was not supplied, was unavailable for the period or was excluded under a documented rule. The source may distinguish those situations with flags or explanatory notes. Do not replace all blanks with zero simply to make a formula run. A supplied zero and an unavailable observation have different evidentiary meanings, and the difference can change a reported summary. Keep the original missing marker and the actual rule used in any calculation.

Fictional case C supplies four intended hourly slots with authored values eight, twelve, twenty and missing. The three known values sum to forty. Their simple arithmetic mean is forty divided by three, while an explicitly different zero-substitution calculation would produce forty divided by four, or ten. Neither calculation is presented as an official air-quality product or a result for real monitoring data. The exercise demonstrates how a denominator and an invented replacement affect a simple summary; it does not justify that replacement.

Three known slots out of four supplied slots gives a known-slot fraction of three quarters. That is not proof that an official daily summary meets its completeness requirements. The exercise does not provide a whole day, an official aggregation rule, instrument-quality information or a NowCast implementation. State those limits with the arithmetic. A reader should not have to infer that a conveniently calculated mean refers only to a tiny authored subset rather than a verified environmental statistic.

Keep verification status separate from physical conditions

A data-status field describes information about a record's handling or interpretation under its source's rules. Read its definition before assigning a meaning. Do not assume a single letter has the same interpretation in every system, and do not borrow a flag legend from a different resource. A status change can require an explanation without demonstrating a change in the underlying physical world. Preserve the status and the version of the record when comparing reports across time.

Fictional case F uses an explicitly invented codebook: P means preliminary and V means verified within this teaching table only. Two snapshots of entry EX-REC-07 retain the same authored numerical value, nine, while the status changes from P to V. The numerical difference is zero. The label change is present in the supplied table, but its cause and process are not supplied. These are not actual county flag codes, evidence of a real review or proof that air conditions improved or deteriorated.

The official county and WPRDC descriptions distinguish unverified data from data reviewed by qualified personnel. That distinction should remain visible when summarizing the described resources. It does not entitle a reader to announce that a particular record was verified without checking that record's actual status. Similarly, an unusual value is not automatically an equipment fault. The county description identifies possible sources of error, not a diagnosis for any individual observation. Keep a question about quality separate from a finding that an error occurred.

Compare reports only after matching their scope

Create a compact comparison note containing the source, product type, station or area, pollutant, measure, unit, observation period, status and version. You may not be able to fill every field immediately. Mark what remains unknown rather than quietly completing it from another website. The note helps identify whether two apparently conflicting values answer the same question. It also records the basis for a later comparison if the source changes, a missing definition is clarified or a different export is selected.

A daily summary and a forecast for the same named area are not competing observations merely because their values differ. One describes a reported period under its own method; the other describes an expectation. A monitor-specific concentration and an area-level index also require different interpretation. Explain the mismatch that you can establish before deciding there is a contradiction. This guide does not reconcile a real pair of air-quality products, establish which source is more accurate or infer the cause of a real disagreement.

If a comparison is appropriate, report the exact operation and the available population. An arithmetic difference between two supplied values does not establish a long-term trend, a causal effect or a neighborhood ranking. A sequence of values needs its own treatment of periods, missing observations, methods and status. Do not make a small illustrative calculation carry a conclusion that requires substantially different evidence. The original cases here demonstrate reading questions and counts, not an environmental study or an assessment of local policy effectiveness.

Use maps and downloaded tables as complementary views

A map can help you discover sites or reporting areas, while a downloaded table can preserve details that a popup abbreviates. Keep the legend, selected layer, filters and time period with a map note. If you capture an image, retain the source link and access time as well. A cropped map or a detached value can lose the definitions needed for interpretation. The separate local-map guide explains that broader issue; this page adds the pollutant, status and reporting-product questions specific to air-monitor information.

For a download, preserve the original header, resource name and any accompanying key. Record the selection you actually made rather than describing the entire catalog as examined. A resource listing can point to hourly, daily, location and historical materials without proving that they share one schema. If a link changes or an older format is labeled as problematic, retain that limitation in the note. This guide accessed catalog descriptions only; it did not download or analyze real county measurement rows.

Do not infer an absent pollutant from its absence in a selected display. A filtered view may intentionally show only certain measures, stations or periods. A blank field may have a documented missing-data meaning. A reporting product may have a purpose narrower than measuring every substance of interest. Identify the display's documented scope before explaining an omission. The fictional exercises contain no findings about pollutants that are present or absent at a real Pittsburgh location.

Keep AI summaries tied to the supplied definitions

An AI-assisted summary can be asked to preserve a report's product type, pollutant, unit, station, time basis, verification status and unresolved fields. Include the source headings and the relevant notes with any extracted values. Ask the draft to distinguish observed labels from proposed interpretations and to leave missing values unresolved. This is an original suggested drafting approach, not a tested prompt or an observed model result. No model was run on an actual air-quality report for this guide.

Review any draft against the supplied material before accepting its wording. Watch for a concentration renamed as AQI, a forecast described as an observation, an hourly label described as today's full-day result or a monitor described as someone's personal exposure. Those are proposed review questions, not claims that a particular tool made those mistakes. The separate Elixir table-meaning guide addresses the broader issue of keeping fields and definitions together. A fluent summary still needs evidence appropriate to the statement it makes.

End with a bounded account of what you read and what remains unanswered. You might have identified a resource, clarified a unit or found that two products use different periods. Those are useful outcomes without a current conditions or health finding. Direct questions about official reporting definitions to the relevant source information, and use official current guidance for health-related decisions. Do not replace an unavailable official interpretation with an invented category, an improvised AQI or a confident statement about a place you have not assessed.

Frequently asked questions

Is a concentration reading the same thing as an AQI number?

No. Preserve the measure label and unit. Fictional case A deliberately gives two fields the same numeral, twelve, while one is a PM2.5 concentration and the other is labeled AQI. No conversion is supplied or performed. The case establishes no current concentration, category or health conclusion. Consult the definition of the actual official product rather than assigning index meaning to a bare number.

Does a freshly updated webpage mean every observation is current?

No. Separate the observation period, report time and your access time. Fictional case B's two declared UTC clock labels are seventy-five minutes apart, but that is not a measured reporting delay for a real service. Check the actual field definitions and time basis before describing a reading as current or comparing it with another report. A page-level update label alone does not settle those questions.

Can missing hourly values be replaced with zero?

Do not make that substitution without an applicable rule and a clear account of its effect. In fictional case C the known-value mean is forty thirds, while the different zero-substitution calculation gives ten. Neither is an official county summary or NowCast. The missing slot remains missing in the supplied evidence, and three quarters known slots is not a finding about official completeness or semantic coverage.

Can one monitor establish the air conditions at my address?

This guide makes no such determination. A monitor identifier, reporting area and personal location describe different scopes. Keep the official product's geographic meaning with its value and consult current official reporting and guidance for the actual question. The invented station rows do not measure a home, route, workplace or individual exposure, and the guide does not provide a personal health or activity assessment.

Does a data-status change mean the air changed?

Not by itself. Fictional case F retains the same numerical value while an invented status changes. Its numerical difference is zero, and the cause of the label change is not supplied. The example's P and V are not official county codes. For a real resource, read its own flag key and record status without inferring a physical improvement, equipment problem or completed review that has not been established.

Are the practice values real Pittsburgh readings?

No. They are original fictional teaching inputs, including invented dates, station identifiers and flags. Their checks cover narrow arithmetic, labels and counts only. No actual monitoring record, sensor, household, air sample, forecast, regulatory determination, medical assessment or AI model was evaluated. Use official sources for current reporting and health-related guidance rather than treating these practice values as environmental evidence.
