Carnegie Library Business Research: Turn a Market Question into a Search Plan

Prepare a focused research request using Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh business resources, with geography, dates and assumptions clearly defined.

AI-assisted editorial guide · Published November 20, 2026

Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh’s business-resource guide points readers toward industry and market research, business planning, and help locating business information. For a Pittsburgh owner, the useful starting point is a precise research question. A database result can support a decision, but it does not establish customer demand for a particular shop, service or price. Use the library’s current resource and contact pages to confirm access and assistance.

Write a decision before collecting statistics

Describe what the business needs to decide and when. A proposed service territory, a new customer segment and a possible storefront require different evidence. For example, an owner considering delivery from a South Side kitchen might ask how to describe the intended service area and compare available market information. Keep the question separate from the conclusion the owner hopes to reach. That makes it easier for a librarian or research helper to suggest useful sources.

Define the place and population

Specify the geography and the people or organizations being counted. A city total, an Allegheny County figure and a small service radius cannot be substituted without explanation. Record whether a number describes residents, households, establishments or employees. If the resource cannot support the desired small-area detail, preserve that limitation rather than dividing a regional number into an invented neighborhood estimate. Ask which geographic definitions the source actually uses.

Keep a source-and-date ledger

For each useful result, save the source name, measurement period, retrieval date, definition and any access limitations. Separate a forecast from an observed count. A recent download can contain older underlying data, so the download date alone does not describe freshness. Keep enough citation detail to reproduce the search. Follow the resource’s permitted-use rules before redistributing extracts or placing licensed data in a public business plan.

Compare like measures before making a chart

Check whether two figures use the same time period, categories and units. If one source counts employers and another counts locations, explain the difference. Do not describe a mismatch as market growth without evidence. A small comparison table with definitions often reveals more than a polished chart. The owner can then identify which apparent opportunity rests on comparable information and which still needs a customer interview or another source.

Separate desk research from customer evidence

Library research can help frame a market, but it does not replace testing the actual offer. For the delivery example, broad population data would not establish willingness to order, acceptable prices or delivery economics. Record those as separate questions for appropriate research. Keep interview observations and transaction evidence labeled with their own limitations. Avoid turning a handful of enthusiastic comments into a statistical estimate of the whole neighborhood.

Bring back a concise unresolved question

After reviewing the initial material, return with the specific gap rather than asking for every available business statistic. Identify what was searched, which definition caused difficulty and what decision remains open. Keep the research file with the planning version it informed. The result should be a transparent evidence trail that another person can inspect, not a claim that the library endorses the business or guarantees a successful launch.

Prepare your evidence and next question

  • Decision and deadline
  • Geographic and customer definitions
  • Source, measurement period and retrieval date
  • Access and redistribution limitations
  • Unresolved question for further research
